## 2.1 Technology adoption and the limits of an evaluation-centered account

Research on individual technology acceptance has provided the dominant vocabulary for explaining why people adopt or reject new systems. The technology acceptance model (Davis, 1989) proposes that perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use drive intention to use, and the unified theory of acceptance and use of technology (Venkatesh et al., 2003) consolidates the field around performance expectancy, effort expectancy, social influence, and facilitating conditions. These frameworks remain productive for studying generative AI, and the present paper does not dispute their predictive value. It does, however, question a scope condition they share. Adoption models treat the user as an individual who evaluates a technology's attributes against a frame of reference that is taken as already fixed. They presuppose that the user has answered a prior question---*what is this technology, and what does it mean for me?*---and then rates the technology's usefulness and ease of use in light of that settled understanding.

In generative AI implementation, that prior question is precisely what is unsettled, and two features of the technology make it consequential. First, generative AI's outputs overlap with the identity-central tasks of knowledge work---drafting, analysis, summarization, coding, advising---so its "usefulness" is double-edged. A system that is highly useful at an employee's core tasks is, by that very fact, evidence that those tasks can be accomplished without the employee. This inverts a foundational assumption of adoption research: where usefulness is normally an adoption-promoting attribute, here high perceived usefulness can be the specific stimulus that raises role threat. Second, implementation is an organizational event, not merely the arrival of a tool; it carries meanings about managerial intent, about the future value of expertise, and about who the organization believes it will need. Mainstream adoption models are not aimed at specifying how these meanings form; they take the user's interpretive frame of reference as given rather than as an outcome to be explained. That scope choice becomes consequential in generative AI implementation, where an account resting on usefulness alone can misread the very cases in which usefulness is being read as replaceability. This is why the paper locates an interpretive, meaning-formation process *upstream* of attribute evaluation, and why it turns to the technological frames and sensemaking traditions to characterize that process.
