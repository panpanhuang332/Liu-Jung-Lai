## 5.2 Boundary conditions and scope

The theory is deliberately scoped. Its mechanisms are strongest where role consequences are uncertain, where the technology's outputs overlap with identity-central knowledge work, and where the technology is queryable, demonstrable, and privately testable; where these conditions are weak---automation peripheral to role identity, or non-interactive systems---both mechanisms attenuate. The three boundary conditions also help delimit the theory rather than serving as incidental moderators: organizational trust shapes the likelihood and strength of narrative reversal (P3); secondary appraisal resources govern which response mode a given threat produces (P8); and AI-generated cue credibility governs whether a self-generated cue moves an appraisal within the loop (P5). Finally, the account concerns interpreted rather than objective substitution: it explains how employees construct the meaning of implementation, not whether generative AI in fact performs their tasks, and its claims should be read within that boundary. A further layer of scope concerns the institutional and occupational context, which the framework's baseline probabilities implicitly presuppose. The mechanisms are articulated with a flexible, knowledge-work labor market in view. Employment protection legislation, collective representation, and occupational licensure systematically alter both the credibility of the substitution reading and the resources available in secondary appraisal: where dismissal is legally difficult, unions are present, or licensure walls off task domains, the AI-as-substitute frame is less plausible on its face and coping resources are institutionally supplemented, lowering the baseline probability of reversal (P2) and shifting the distribution of threat-driven modes (P8) away from avoidance and defensive adoption; where employment is at-will and roles are unlicensed, the reverse holds. These institutional parameters are best treated as context factors that calibrate the propositions' baseline probabilities rather than as additional constructs, and comparative designs across institutional regimes and occupational categories are a natural extension of the empirical agenda below. The framework is therefore not intended as a general theory of all AI implementation, but of implementations in which generative AI is interpreted through role consequences.
