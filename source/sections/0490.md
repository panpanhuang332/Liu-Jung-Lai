## 5.4 Practical implications

Because positive communication is not received as intended when employees perceive role vulnerability or low trust, and because employees will in any case generate their own role-relevant evidence by testing the technology, generative AI implementation is as much a matter of meaning management as of technical readiness or user training. The framework cautions, however, against reducing this to communication tactics: its mechanisms imply that trust, genuine participation, and consistency between managerial words and observable deeds shape interpretation more durably than message wording, and that the recursive loop places part of the interpretive process beyond managerial control.

Three implications follow. First, reassurance alone is insufficient: messages emphasizing support and augmentation should be paired with specificity about task scope, role redesign, participation, and observable commitments, since vague reassurance can make substitution more rather than less salient. Second, because employees will privately test the system, organizations cannot fully control the loop but can shape it---through guided experimentation, role-specific use cases, and structured occasions to interpret collectively what AI demonstrations do and do not imply for role value. Third, organizations should diagnose the mode of adoption rather than infer success from usage volume: high use may reflect defensive adoption rather than genuine integration, and low use may reflect avoidance or a compliance-only minimum, so implementation metrics should attend to motive, depth, and stability of use.
