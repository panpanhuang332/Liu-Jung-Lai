### 2.5.3 Self-referential cues and the computers-as-social-actors interface

Why should the system's statements *about itself* carry interpretive weight, rather than being processed as inert output? The computers-are-social-actors paradigm provides the answer. Nass et al. (1994), Reeves and Nass (1996), and Nass and Moon (2000) show that people apply social rules, expectations, and attributions to computers, responding to them socially even while knowing the interlocutor is a machine. Generative AI intensifies this tendency: its fluent, first-person, conversational output invites employees to treat its self-descriptions---"I can draft that report," "here is how I would analyze this"---as quasi-testimonial claims rather than as mere function outputs. **Self-referential cues** therefore enter sensemaking with more weight than an inert tool's specification sheet would command, because the interface encourages processing them as claims made by an agent-like source about tasks the employee owns. This is the theoretical grounding for treating the system's statements about its own capabilities as a distinct and consequential class of role-relevant cue.
