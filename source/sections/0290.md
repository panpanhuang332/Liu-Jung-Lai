### 2.5.6 Socially transmitted cues: the loop is embedded in a social process

The loop as developed so far is dyadic---employee and system---but sensemaking is, in Weick's (1995) account, irreducibly social, and the emerging evidence on generative AI sensemaking is itself collective: Agarwal et al.'s (2026) sensemaking--sensegiving analysis draws on users making meaning publicly, for one another. A third class of cue therefore enters the loop alongside self-referential and demonstrative cues: **socially transmitted cues**---a colleague's demonstration of the system on shared tasks, AI-generated outputs circulating within a team, a manager's own observed use, and stories about who used the system for what. These are, in effect, vicarious demonstrative cues: they carry a demonstration's evidentiary force without requiring the employee's own probe, and they interact with private experimentation rather than merely paralleling it. An impressive circulated output can prompt an employee's own probing; conversely, covert use can diffuse socially precisely because its outputs circulate while their authorship does not (Gillespie et al., 2025), so that private experimentation and social transmission amplify one another. Socially transmitted cues are subject to the same crediting logic as artifact-generated cues, with *source* credibility---the colleague's perceived competence, the manager's perceived candor---playing the role that the machine heuristic plays for the artifact's own outputs. The framework accordingly treats its distinctive contribution, the artifact-generated channel, as embedded within rather than sealed off from this social channel: the propositions in Section 4.3 are stated for artifact-generated cues, the channel prior accounts leave unspecified, with parallel effects expected for socially transmitted cues under source-credibility moderation.
