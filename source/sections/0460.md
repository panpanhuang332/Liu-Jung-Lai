## 5.1 Theoretical contributions

The paper makes exactly three contributions---a mechanism of narrative reversal, a recursive cue-production account of generative AI sensemaking, and a response-mode taxonomy of adoption---and its theoretical apparatus is organized hierarchically in their service: technological frames and sensemaking form the backbone, appraisal and protection motivation theory the bridge, and the remaining literatures supporting materials for specific mechanism components (Section 2). Each contribution is developed below against the literatures it most directly advances; AI-threat research is advanced jointly by the second and third contributions rather than by a separate one.

**Contribution 1: a mechanism of narrative reversal (advancing technological frames and sensegiving research).** Frame research established that managers, technologists, and users hold divergent frames and that incongruence shapes implementation outcomes (Orlikowski & Gash, 1994). The present framework specifies a more specific phenomenon: an explicitly positive managerial frame is not merely read differently but pragmatically reversed into a substitution signal. The contribution is the reversal mechanism itself---the pragmatic and discursive conditions (Section 2.3; P1--P3) under which reassurance, strategic ambiguity, and efficiency vocabulary turn an enablement message into evidence of substitution. The mechanism also sharpens the relation to sensegiving (Gioia & Chittipeddi, 1991). Sensegiving *failure* means the intended frame is not accepted; narrative *backfire* means the intended message is accepted---as evidence for the opposite interpretation. The distinction is consequential because the remedies differ: failure calls for clearer or more persuasive sensegiving, whereas backfire implies that intensified reassurance can worsen the problem (P1, P1a). This extends frame research beyond documenting that frames differ to specifying how a positive frame can invert.

**Contribution 2: a recursive cue-production account of generative AI sensemaking (advancing sensemaking and AI-threat research).** Sensemaking research treats the technology as an object about which sense is made and, in its sensegiving variant, treats managers as the source of framing cues (Weick, 1995; Maitlis & Christianson, 2014). The framework instead theorizes the artifact-generated outputs of the technology as a recursive source of role-relevant cues that employees enact, credit, and appraise against specific role components (Table 2). *Recursive* here means more than *iterative*: the loop does not merely repeat interpretation across successive encounters; each updated appraisal changes the selection of the next probe, so the sampling of evidence is itself endogenous to the appraisal that the evidence feeds (P7). Extending enacted sensemaking (Weick, 1988) to a setting where the artifact generates cues on demand, this is where the recursive account does work that a one-time reception model cannot. The same account advances AI-threat research, which typically begins from perceived threat or treats the technology as a relatively fixed stimulus (Brougham & Haar, 2018; Mirbabaie et al., 2022). Here threat is constructed and revisable: assembled from an organizational narrative, an interpretive frame, self-generated and socially transmitted cues, and the employee's appraisal resources, it can entrench, oscillate, or reverse through the loop (P5--P7)---which explains why the same system produces divergent role appraisals across a workforce, and why those appraisals remain unstable rather than settling.

**Contribution 3: a response-mode view of adoption (advancing technology adoption research).** Adoption models treat perceived usefulness as an adoption-promoting attribute (Davis, 1989; Venkatesh et al., 2003). The framework identifies a scope condition under which this inverts: when the technology's outputs overlap with identity-central tasks, high perceived usefulness can be the specific stimulus that raises role threat---usefulness read as replaceability (Section 2.1; P4, P6). Two departures follow. First, the interpretive step---what the implementation means for the role---is located upstream of attribute evaluation. Second, the outcome is reconceived as a mode of response rather than a level of intention (Section 3.4; P8--P10), because identical usage frequency can arise from entirely different motivations: an employee complying with a mandate and an employee using the system intensively to avoid appearing replaceable may register the same volume of use while enacting different modes with different trajectories and consequences. This also explains why the distinction between compliance-only use and defensive adoption has remained invisible to prior research: adoption studies measure intention or use volume, on which the two modes are observationally equivalent, and defensive use is often deliberately concealed (Gillespie et al., 2025), so it never surfaces in attributed usage metrics. The distinction becomes visible only when driver, depth, and trajectory are measured (P9, P9a)---which is precisely what the taxonomy requires.
