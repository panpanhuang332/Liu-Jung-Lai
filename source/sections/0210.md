### 2.4.1 The dual pathway and the response-mode claim

A frame is not yet an outcome. The movement from the AI-as-substitute frame to an employee response runs through appraisal, and cognitive appraisal theory supplies the structure of that movement. Lazarus and Folkman (1984) distinguish primary appraisal---whether an encounter is relevant to one's well-being and, if so, whether it is benign, harmful, threatening, or challenging---from secondary appraisal---the assessment of the coping resources and options available. Crucially, the same event can be appraised as a *threat* (anticipated harm to valued commitments under insufficient resources) or as a *challenge* (potential for mastery or gain under adequate resources). This grounds the paper's dual pathway. The AI-as-substitute frame biases primary appraisal toward threat, but secondary appraisal---the resources an employee perceives for protecting or adapting the role---shapes whether the encounter is experienced as unmanageable threat, manageable challenge, or an opportunity for role adaptation, and thus whether it stabilizes as **role threat appraisal** or **role opportunity appraisal**. The two appraisals are not free-floating alternatives; they are the threat and challenge poles of a single appraisal process operating on the same interpretive input. In this framework they are not constructs imported alongside appraisal theory but the domain-specific instantiations of its two poles: role threat appraisal is threat appraisal specified to the valued components of one's professional role, and role opportunity appraisal is *challenge appraisal concretized for the role-centered context of generative AI implementation*---the appraisal that those same role components can be extended, elevated, or redefined rather than endangered. Casting the opportunity pathway as challenge appraisal given role-specific content, rather than as the mere absence of threat, is what makes it a genuine competing appraisal rather than a rhetorical counterweight. Within AI research specifically, the dual structure has a direct precedent: Ding (2021) shows that employees' STARA awareness can be appraised as a hindrance or as a challenge, with the two appraisals predicting divergent engagement outcomes. The present framework builds on that precedent while departing from it on three points: the appraisal here is tied to a specific interpretive event---this implementation, framed by this narrative---rather than to generalized technological awareness; it is componential, specifying which role components are endangered or enabled rather than yielding a global valence; and it is recursively revisable through the employee's own interaction with the system rather than measured as a standing disposition.

Protection motivation theory extends this structure in a way that directly grounds the paper's response-mode claim. Rogers (1975; see also Maddux & Rogers, 1983) holds that responses to threat depend jointly on threat appraisal (perceived severity and vulnerability) and coping appraisal (response efficacy and self-efficacy): high threat combined with high coping efficacy tends to produce adaptive, problem-focused responses, whereas high threat combined with low coping efficacy tends to produce maladaptive responses such as avoidance, denial, and wishful thinking. This is the theoretical hinge for the argument that role threat does not translate into a single, lower level of adoption. An employee who appraises high threat but possesses coping resources may respond by *intensifying* engagement---learning and deploying generative AI to remain indispensable, an adaptive and self-protective response the paper terms defensive adoption---while an employee with the same threat but few resources may withdraw. Protection motivation theory thus accounts for the threat-driven responses---avoidance under low coping efficacy, defensive adoption under high coping efficacy---but does not by itself yield genuine integration, which the framework traces to the opportunity pathway. The outcome of threat is therefore a *kind* of response selected by coping appraisal, not a scalar depression of intention; this is the appraisal-theoretic basis for reframing adoption as a mode of response rather than a level. The behavioral distinction between defensive adoption and genuine integration---which can resemble each other in volume while differing in driver, depth, and stability---is formalized, with the full taxonomy, in Section 3.4.
