# 1. Introduction

Organizations often regard a generative artificial intelligence (AI) implementation as successful when its use ceases to be discretionary and becomes structural: standardized for repeated use, interoperable with existing systems, and embedded across sequential workflows. Employees may read the same features differently—as evidence that the work no longer depends on the person who currently performs it. The organizational reading is well grounded. Implementation research has long treated routinization and infusion, rather than initial acceptance, as the mature stages of technology assimilation (Cooper & Zmud, 1990; Purvis et al., 2001; Vial, 2019), and field evidence indicates that the productivity gains of generative AI materialize most reliably when its use is embedded in structured work processes rather than left to ad hoc adoption (Brynjolfsson et al., 2025; Noy & Zhang, 2023). What remains insufficiently theorized is the employee reading: how employees infer incumbent dependence from the structural features of a specific AI deployment.

This study examines that inference. AI workflow integration depth refers to the extent to which AI use is routinized, standardized, connected to organizational systems, and embedded across interdependent workflow stages. These structural features can make work processes more replicable and scalable across organizational settings—in part by reducing the extent to which workflow execution depends on a particular incumbent. Research on technological frames and sensemaking has established that members interpret technology through the concrete arrangements in which it arrives, not merely through official accounts of it (Orlikowski & Gash, 1994; Weick, 1995). When employees read a deeply integrated deployment, they may therefore infer that the role’s execution has become less dependent on a specific incumbent. This study labels that inference perceived role replaceability. The same structural features thus carry two signals at once: they signal implementation success from an organizational-performance standpoint while signaling role replaceability from an incumbent-role standpoint. This study terms this simultaneous dual signaling from a common structural source the integration–replaceability paradox.

Perceived role replaceability is defined as a non-evaluative inference that the core outputs and decisions associated with a role can be reproduced with reduced dependence on the current role holder. It does not by itself imply that the organization intends to eliminate the role or that the employee anticipates personal harm. This delimitation separates it from its nearest neighbors. Job insecurity is a perceived threat to the continuity of one’s employment (Greenhalgh & Rosenblatt, 1984; Shoss, 2017), and STARA awareness is a generalized belief that smart technologies could affect one’s career prospects (Brougham & Haar, 2018); both concern anticipated personal outcomes, whereas replaceability concerns the technical structure of the role. It is likewise distinct from the AI-as-substitute frame, an employee’s strategic reading that the organization is absorbing or displacing roles through AI (Lai, under review), and from role threat appraisal, the evaluation that valued role components—employment security, the market value of expertise, role autonomy, and occupational identity—are endangered (Lai, under review; Lazarus & Folkman, 1984). An employee may judge a role technically reproducible yet feel personally secure, or the reverse. Keeping the inference apart from the appraisal is what makes the paradox theoretically productive: it opens the question of when technical replaceability is, and is not, translated into perceived role damage.

Existing literature recognizes tensions surrounding AI in organizations but locates them elsewhere. The automation–augmentation literature frames the tension as a managerial design choice between substituting for and complementing human work (Raisch & Krakowski, 2021), and studies of algorithmic management examine how technologies reconfigure control (Kellogg et al., 2020). Closest to the present concern, Lai (under review) theorizes how explicitly positive enablement narratives can be pragmatically reversed into an AI-as-substitute frame. What these streams leave unaddressed is a dual-signal mechanism located in the deployment’s structure itself: why the structural markers of successful implementation can become replaceability cues even when no narrative misstep occurs. This study accordingly extends Lai’s framework from the narrative layer to the structural layer.

The research question is: how does deep AI workflow integration simultaneously generate perceptions of implementation success and role replaceability, and under what conditions does perceived replaceability translate into role threat? The expected mechanism is that integration depth raises perceived organizational implementation success and, in parallel, perceived role replaceability; replaceability in turn raises role threat appraisal; and a credible role-redesign commitment weakens this latter translation. Formal hypotheses are derived in Section 3.

The boundary condition warrants introduction here. The theoretical construct is credible role-redesign commitment; what the experiment manipulates is its substantiation. Both commitment conditions promise the same role-redesign content—retraining, retained human decision responsibilities, and new career paths; what varies is whether these assurances are backed by verifiable evidence: approved budgets, specified dates, formal policy documents, designated accountable managers, and auditable progress. Drawing on signaling theory and behavioral integrity research (Connelly et al., 2011; Simons, 2002), specific, costly, and verifiable commitments are informative where general assurances are not. Critically, a substantiated commitment does not dispute what the technology can do; it supplies evidence about consequences, weakening the link between the inference that the role is technically reproducible and the appraisal that one’s valued role components are thereby endangered.

To examine the mechanism, this study designs a 2 × 2 between-subjects scenario experiment with staged presentation among working adults in knowledge-intensive occupations. Participants first read the integration manipulation (low vs. high)—describing only structural features such as process coverage, standardized specifications, system connections, and formal tracking, without stating that work can be transferred across employees—and then report perceived organizational implementation success and perceived role replaceability. After a brief filler task that separates the mediator assessment from the subsequent material to reduce hypothesis guessing and measurement-induced carryover, participants read the commitment manipulation (low vs. high substantiation) and then report perceived commitment credibility, which serves as the manipulation check, and role threat appraisal. This sequencing is designed so that replaceability judgments are formed from structure alone, before any commitment information, and so that the commitment operates on the translation of that inference rather than on the inference itself. Hypothesized effects are tested through analysis of variance and bootstrapped moderated mediation analysis, with the randomized substantiation condition as the moderator.

The study intends three contributions. First, it retheorizes AI workflow integration as a dual signal, extending explanations of adverse employee response beyond communication failure to the structural characteristics of implementation itself. Second, it introduces and delimits perceived role replaceability as a non-evaluative inference about the role’s dependence on its incumbent, specifying a previously omitted inferential step between structural integration and role threat appraisal. Third, it locates the operative site of credible role-redesign commitment: not in persuading employees that AI cannot perform their tasks, but in governing whether acknowledged technical replaceability is translated into anticipated role loss.

# 2. Theoretical Background

## 2.1 AI implementation success and workflow integration

Implementation research has long distinguished the decision to adopt a technology from the organizational work of making it consequential. In stage models of information technology implementation, acceptance is followed by routinization, in which use becomes a normal and administratively supported part of ongoing operations, and by infusion, in which the technology penetrates work processes deeply enough to be used to its fullest potential (Cooper & Zmud, 1990). Assimilation research likewise treats the extent to which a technology spreads across work processes and becomes embedded in organizational routines—rather than the formal fact of adoption—as the substantive criterion of implementation progress (Purvis et al., 2001), and digital transformation scholarship draws the same conclusion at a larger scale: value arises from structural change in how work is organized, not from the presence of new tools (Vial, 2019).

Organizational deployment of generative AI increasingly presents the same assimilation challenge. Evidence from experimental tasks and large-scale workplace deployment indicates that generative AI can improve worker productivity under structured task and organizational conditions (Brynjolfsson et al., 2025; Noy & Zhang, 2023). Realizing such gains at the organizational level, however, requires moving beyond isolated tool use toward routinized and coordinated deployment (Cooper & Zmud, 1990; Purvis et al., 2001): prompts and outputs are standardized into specifications and templates, the system is connected to internal data sources and downstream platforms, and its use is embedded as a formal step across interdependent stages of a workflow, with review and version tracking attached.

This study summarizes such efforts in the construct of AI workflow integration depth: the extent to which AI use is routinized, standardized, connected to organizational systems, and embedded across interdependent workflow stages. Two clarifications delimit the construct. First, integration depth is a property of the deployment, not of the technology: the same model can sit at either end of the continuum depending on how the organization arranges its use. Second, the frequently cited payoffs of integration—replicability of processes across settings and scalability of output—are consequences that the structure can make possible, not additional defining dimensions.

The corresponding employee-side judgment is perceived organizational implementation success: an employee’s higher-order judgment that the AI deployment has become operationally sustainable, institutionalized, scalable, and capable of generating consistent organizational value. It is an evaluation of the deployment’s organizational maturity and consequences, rather than a restatement of its observable integration features—a distinction that also disciplines measurement, since items assessing whether the system “is standardized” would capture the manipulation, not the judgment.

## 2.2 From workflow integration to perceived role replaceability

Why should employees read the structure of a deployment as bearing on incumbent dependence? Two literatures, taken together, supply the mechanism.

The first explains what integration can do to knowledge. Research on organizational capability treats codification as the conversion of personal, partly tacit knowledge into organizational property: when know-how is articulated into specifications, templates, and documented procedures, it becomes teachable, storable, and usable by others (Kogut & Zander, 1992). Research on routines makes the complementary point that recurring, standardized action patterns function as repositories of organizational memory that can persist through changes in the individuals who enact them (Feldman & Pentland, 2003; Nelson & Winter, 1982). Deep AI workflow integration can function as a form of partial codification. By externalizing portions of a role’s recurring judgment sequence into specifications, embedded prompts, documented hand-offs, and tracked revisions, integration can make workflow execution less dependent on knowledge held exclusively by a particular incumbent. This does not imply that all tacit judgment is eliminated—codification is rarely complete, and integrated deployments may even deepen reliance on those who design and oversee them. What it implies is weaker and sufficient: reduced incumbent dependence becomes a plausible inference from the observable structure of the deployment.

The second literature explains how employees come to draw it. Work on technological frames and sensemaking shows that organizational members interpret a technology from the concrete arrangements in which it reaches them—what it is connected to, what procedures surround it, what its use displaces—rather than solely from official accounts of its purpose (Orlikowski & Gash, 1994; Weick, 1995). Employees inside an integrated deployment encounter its structural features directly and continuously: they follow the standardized specification, submit to the tracked workflow, and observe that their outputs are increasingly governed by shared specifications rather than by wholly individualized working practices. The structure itself is cue material, and these cues make perceived role replaceability—the non-evaluative inference, defined in Section 1, that the core outputs and decisions associated with the role can be reproduced with reduced dependence on its current holder—a systematic and plausible inference. Table 1 summarizes how this inference differs from its nearest conceptual neighbors; the constructs differ in referent, logical form, and whether an interpretation of organizational intent or an anticipated adverse personal impact is part of their content. Two employees can share an identical replaceability inference about the same deployment and diverge entirely in what they conclude for themselves. That divergence is the subject of Section 2.4; the prior question is why the inference and the success judgment arrive together.

## 2.3 The integration–replaceability paradox

Paradox, in the organizational literature, denotes contradictory yet interrelated elements that exist simultaneously and persist over time (Smith & Lewis, 2011). The duality identified here can be located against each part of that definition, and doing so fixes what the empirical study can and cannot claim.

The two signals are contradictory in what they affirm, and the contradiction is relational rather than affective: the same structure that increases the organization’s capacity to reproduce work can reduce the structural centrality of any specific incumbent. Read from an organizational-performance standpoint, routinized, standardized, connected, and embedded AI use indicates a matured implementation; read from an incumbent-role standpoint, the same features constitute evidence that the role’s execution has become reproducible with less dependence on this particular person. The first reading affirms organizational reproducibility; the second indicates reduced incumbent centrality. Neither reading is itself an appraisal of harm—that step belongs to Section 2.4—but the two cannot rise together without tension, because what the first celebrates as organizational capacity is constituted in part by what the second registers as diminished individual indispensability.

The two signals are interrelated because they arise from a common structural source. The routinization and codification that support replicability can also provide evidence of reduced incumbent dependence (Kogut & Zander, 1992). Organizations may attenuate the second signal through workflow design—retaining exception handling, keeping consequential judgment outside the standardized flow—but they cannot assume that integration will be read solely as an organizational benefit, because the replaceability inference is grounded in observable properties of the deployment rather than arising solely from a communication misunderstanding. This common-source character distinguishes the present paradox from a generic trade-off between separable features, and also from the automation–augmentation paradox, which concerns competing managerial logics for allocating tasks between humans and machines (Raisch & Krakowski, 2021); the paradox here concerns two simultaneous interpretations of the same realized deployment structure.

The tension is theoretically persistent insofar as the structural cues remain present after implementation: because the inference has an evidentiary basis in the deployment itself, it is governable but unlikely to be fully eliminated through communication alone. The present experiment does not test longitudinal persistence; it tests the initial dual-perception signature implied by the paradox. That signature is a dual-effect claim: empirical support for the paradox requires evidence that deep integration raises both perceived organizational implementation success and perceived role replaceability. Evidence of a threat effect alone would document a cost of integration, not a paradox; evidence of the success effect alone would document assimilation, not a tension. The joint pattern is the initial empirical signature examined in this study, and Section 3 states it as two co-equal hypotheses.

## 2.4 Perceived role replaceability and role threat appraisal

Whether the replaceability inference matters for the employee is a question of appraisal. In the cognitive appraisal tradition, primary appraisal evaluates whether an encounter is relevant to one’s well-being and, if so, whether it portends harm, threat, or challenge; threat denotes anticipated harm to something the person values (Lazarus & Folkman, 1984). Applied to a specific AI implementation, role threat appraisal is the evaluation that valued aspects of one’s role are endangered, which may include employment security, the market value of expertise, role autonomy, and occupational identity and status (Lai, under review).

Perceived role replaceability supplies the content on which this appraisal operates. When employees interpret reproducibility as enabling headcount logic, the inference implicates employment security; when they interpret the externalization of expertise into templates as reducing its scarcity, it implicates the market value of their skills; when they understand standardization as relocating judgment into procedure, it can be appraised as narrowing autonomy; and when completing work to a template is read as eroding what made the role distinctively theirs, it implicates occupational identity. In each case the threat runs through the employee’s interpretation of the replaceability inference, not from the deployment structure directly; the general expectation is therefore a positive association between the inference and role threat appraisal, with replaceability as the mediating step.

The association is a translation rather than an entailment, and the constructs’ different logical forms explain why. Replaceability is descriptive and role-referential: it characterizes the technical structure of a position. Threat is evaluative and self-referential: it characterizes anticipated harm to the person occupying the position. Between the two stands everything that appraisal theory places in the person and the situation—which aspects the employee values most, and the secondary appraisal of resources and options such as tenure protections, credentials, mobility, or the realistic prospect of moving into work the deployment cannot absorb. An employee may fully accept that the role is technically reproducible and yet appraise little threat. It is precisely this contingency that creates the theoretical space for an organizational boundary condition: if the inference-to-appraisal step depended only on the inference, nothing the organization did after integrating could matter.

## 2.5 Credible role-redesign commitment as a boundary condition

The boundary condition examined here is the credible role-redesign commitment introduced in Section 1: verifiable organizational actions indicating how responsibilities, judgment authority, skills, career paths, and use of time will be reconfigured after integration. Its theoretical mechanics rest primarily on signaling theory, with behavioral integrity research supplying a complementary logic.

Employees evaluating an implementation face an information asymmetry: the consequences that matter to them lie in the organization’s future conduct, which cannot be observed at the time of appraisal. Signaling theory holds that under such asymmetry, receivers discriminate among senders by the cost and observability of their signals, because signals that are cheap to send are available alike to senders who will and will not follow through (Connelly et al., 2011; Spence, 1973). General assurances—that employees will be supported, that the company values its people—are cheap in exactly this sense. A commitment acquires credibility from its substantiation: approved budgets, specified dates, formal policy documents, designated accountable managers, and progress that can be audited. These devices are more organizationally constraining, more costly to reverse, and more readily verifiable in the present, which is what allows them to carry information about the future; they also provide a prospective basis for judging whether organizational words are likely to be matched by deeds, the alignment that behavioral integrity research identifies as an important basis for trust in management (Simons, 2002).

The operative site of such a commitment is the translation identified in Section 2.4, not the inference that precedes it. Its primary theorized function is not to alter the perceived replaceability of the currently integrated workflow: that workflow remains codified, connected, and reproducible, and the commitment does not dispute it. What the commitment supplies is evidence that the employee will occupy a reconfigured role whose value and continuity do not depend solely on that workflow—retained judgment authority addressing autonomy, funded retraining with timelines addressing the market value of expertise, specified career paths addressing employment security and occupational identity. The same replaceability inference is then appraised against a different anticipated future, weakening its translation into role threat. This placement completes the argument of Section 2.3: because the commitment operates downstream of the inference, it is the instrument by which the paradox is governed, even as the inference itself remains available to be drawn.

# 3. Hypothesis Development and Research Model

## 3.1 The dual-perception signature of integration (H1a and H1b)

Section 2.1 established that routinization, standardization, system connection, and cross-stage embedding are the structural markers by which implementation research identifies a mature deployment (Cooper & Zmud, 1990). For employees who encounter these markers, they are also the raw material for a higher-order judgment about the deployment’s organizational standing: a workflow that runs to specification, connects to the organization’s systems, and carries formal review and tracking is likely to be judged as sustainable, institutionalized, and capable of generating consistent value. The first expectation is therefore that employees exposed to a deeply integrated deployment will judge the implementation more successful in these organizational terms than employees exposed to a discretionary, tool-like deployment.

**H1a.** Higher AI workflow integration depth increases perceived organizational implementation success.

Section 2.2 established that the same markers function as codification cues: externalizing portions of recurring judgment sequences into specifications, embedded prompts, documented hand-offs, and tracked revisions can convert parts of individually held know-how into organizationally accessible resources (Kogut & Zander, 1992), making reduced incumbent dependence a plausible inference from observable structure. Employees encountering such an arrangement may infer that the relevant outputs are governed by shared specifications rather than by wholly individualized practice. The second expectation is therefore that the same manipulation elevates the replaceability inference.

**H1b.** Higher AI workflow integration depth increases perceived role replaceability.

H1a and H1b are stated as co-equal hypotheses because together they constitute the initial empirical signature of the integration–replaceability paradox defined in Section 2.3. They are conceptually distinct and separately evaluated hypotheses: support for either alone would not support the paradox claim. An integration effect on replaceability without a success effect would document a replaceability inference without the corresponding implementation-success signal; a success effect without a replaceability effect would document assimilation without tension. Only the joint pattern—both perceptions elevated by the same manipulated structure—constitutes the initial empirical signature of dual signaling from a common structural source.

## 3.2 From the replaceability inference to role threat appraisal (H2 and H3)

Section 2.4 located the connection between the two focal constructs in appraisal: consistent with appraisal theory, a descriptive inference becomes threatening when it is evaluated against valued role aspects and available coping resources (Lazarus & Folkman, 1984). Perceived role replaceability supplies content that can be so evaluated—through headcount logic, reduced scarcity of expertise, relocated judgment, and eroded distinctiveness. Because these implications arise when the employee interprets the inference self-referentially, the expected association is positive.

**H2.** Perceived role replaceability is positively associated with role threat appraisal.

If integration elevates the replaceability inference (H1b) and the inference is appraised as threatening (H2), the two links compose an indirect pathway from structure to threat. This is the previously omitted inferential step identified in Section 1: the focal theorized pathway from integration depth to role threat operates through what employees infer about incumbent dependence. This claim does not require the indirect pathway to be exclusive; the analysis will estimate total, direct, and indirect effects, and residual direct effects may indicate additional mechanisms beyond perceived role replaceability.

**H3.** AI workflow integration depth has a positive indirect effect on role threat appraisal through perceived role replaceability.

## 3.3 The boundary condition: commitment substantiation (H4 and H5)

Section 2.5 argued that the translation from inference to appraisal—not the inference itself—is where organizational action can intervene. A role-redesign commitment whose content is substantiated by approved budgets, specified dates, formal policy documents, designated accountable managers, and auditable progress is organizationally constraining, costly to reverse, and verifiable in the present; on signaling logic, it therefore carries information about the organization’s future conduct that unsubstantiated assurances of identical content do not (Connelly et al., 2011), and it offers a prospective basis for expecting words to be matched by deeds (Simons, 2002). An employee holding a given replaceability inference then appraises it against a different anticipated future: retained judgment authority, funded retraining, and specified career paths address the role aspects through which replaceability would otherwise be read as threatening. The commitment disputes nothing about what the technology can do; it changes what the inference is expected to lead to. The prediction is a weakened inference-to-threat translation under high substantiation.

**H4.** Role-redesign commitment substantiation moderates the positive relationship between perceived role replaceability and role threat appraisal, such that the relationship is weaker under high substantiation than under low substantiation.

Combining H3 and H4 yields the integrated claim: if the indirect pathway from integration to threat runs through the replaceability inference, and the final link of that pathway weakens under high substantiation, then the indirect effect itself is conditional on the commitment condition. This is a second-stage moderated mediation structure, with the randomized substantiation condition as the moderator.

**H5.** The conditional indirect effect of AI workflow integration depth on role threat appraisal through perceived role replaceability is smaller under high commitment substantiation than under low substantiation.

Solid arrows denote hypothesized directional effects; the dashed arrow denotes moderation. The direct path from integration depth to role threat appraisal is estimated in the mediation analysis but is omitted from the figure because no directional hypothesis is proposed for that path.

Two further absences are deliberate. No path from commitment substantiation to perceived role replaceability is hypothesized or tested in the present design, because replaceability is assessed before the commitment information is introduced; the model therefore concerns the commitment’s downstream role in appraisal rather than any possible post-commitment revision of the replaceability inference. No arrow connects perceived organizational implementation success to the threat pathway: the success judgment is a parallel outcome that constitutes the paradox jointly with H1b, not a step in the threat mechanism. All constructs are individual-level perceptions reported by the participant; the two factors are randomized between-subjects conditions.

In summary, the model holds that a single manipulated structure produces two simultaneous perceptions (H1a, H1b); that one of them—the replaceability inference—is positively associated with role threat appraisal (H2) and carries the structure’s indirect effect on threat (H3); and that substantiated role redesign weakens that translation (H4) and thereby the indirect effect itself (H5), governing the paradox at the point where inference becomes appraisal.

# 4. Method

## 4.1 Research design

This study will employ a 2 × 2 between-subjects scenario experiment with staged presentation and staged randomization. The first factor, AI workflow integration depth (low vs. high), will be randomly assigned before Stage 1 of the scenario. The second factor, role-redesign commitment substantiation (low vs. high), will be randomly assigned only after participants complete Stage 1 and a neutral filler task, in equal proportions within each integration condition, yielding a final 2 × 2 factorial structure. Perceived organizational implementation success and perceived role replaceability will be assessed after Stage 1, before any commitment information exists for the participant; role threat appraisal will be assessed after Stage 2. This sequencing operationalizes the model’s core claim: the commitment is theorized to act on the translation of the replaceability inference into threat, not on the inference itself, and Stage 1 outcomes cannot be influenced by a manipulation that has not yet been assigned.

## 4.2 Participants and sampling

Participants will be working adults enrolled in a continuing-education degree program. Inclusion criteria are: age 18 or older; current paid employment of at least 20 hours per week; current work involving at least one of analysis, reporting, information processing, professional judgment, or client communication; ability to read the scenario language; and no participation in any preliminary study. Familiarity with generative AI is not an inclusion criterion, to avoid selection bias; prior generative AI use will be measured for descriptive and robustness purposes. Because participants belong to course cohorts, cohorts will serve as randomization blocks: random assignment will be conducted within cohorts, data collection will occur within a short window, recruitment materials will ask participants not to discuss the study before its completion, and cohort membership will be recorded for inclusion in the analysis models.

Sample size will be determined in two steps. An a priori G*Power analysis (Faul et al., 2007; f = .25, α = .05, power = .80) establishes a floor of approximately 128 participants for the factorial main effects; because the focal conditional indirect effect (H5) is not adequately powered by this omnibus calculation, the final recruitment target will be determined through a Monte Carlo power simulation for the second-stage moderated mediation model. Effect-size inputs will be informed by the material pretest but anchored to preregistered smallest effects of theoretical interest and examined across conservative sensitivity ranges. The preregistration will specify the simulation’s target power, the assumed a₁, b₁, and b₃ values and their sources, expected reliabilities, the anticipated exclusion rate, balanced allocation, the number of simulation repetitions, the index of moderated mediation as the primary power criterion, and the simulation code. Recruitment and valid-sample targets will be reported separately.

Two analytic samples will be distinguished. H1a and H1b will use all preregistered-valid participants who complete the Stage 1 outcomes and the filler-task validity checks, including participants who subsequently discontinue before assignment to the Stage 2 manipulation; H2–H5 will use preregistered-valid participants who additionally complete the Stage 2 manipulation and the role-threat outcome. Attrition before Stage 2 assignment will be reported by integration condition; attrition afterward will be reported by full X × W condition. Exclusions will follow preregistered rules only: failure of the instructed-response attention check; fewer than two of four filler comprehension items correct, judged on the four-item total and never on a single error; scenario-page reading time below a pretest-derived minimum combined with total completion time below one-third of the sample median, with each criterion also applied singly in sensitivity analyses; and duplicate or invalid submissions. Exclusion reasons may be multiply coded with unique participants counted once, and exclusions will be documented by condition and tested for differential attrition. Under a randomization-preserving analysis principle, all randomized participants meeting these validity criteria will be analyzed under their assigned conditions; no participant will be excluded on the basis of manipulation-check or diagnostic-check scores.

## 4.3 Experimental scenarios and manipulations

All participants will read a common introduction describing a fictional mid-sized professional services firm, the participant’s role as a project analyst meeting performance expectations, and a company-approved generative AI system whose capability, output quality, supervisor support, and stated purpose are held constant. The integration manipulation varies only structural features: a stand-alone, discretionary assistant for occasional discrete tasks, unconnected to company systems and without shared templates, versus a system embedded as a formal step across connected stages from intake to archiving, governed by standardized specifications and company-maintained templates, connected to internal databases, and subject to version tracking described as serving quality purposes. Neither condition states that work can be transferred across employees, and terms such as replace, layoff, or redundant appear nowhere, so that any replaceability judgment arises as the participant’s own inference. The substantiation manipulation holds the three core commitments and their modal strength constant across conditions—retraining, retained final approval responsibility for client recommendations, and a senior analyst career path—while varying the evidence that substantiates those commitments: an approved budget, specified timelines, revised job descriptions, designated accountable managers, quarterly progress reports, and a published policy document, versus the same three commitments in a general message of approximately matched length. The two messages are designed to be equivalent in tone and positivity; intended equivalence on output quality and message tone, and acceptable limits on monitoring perceptions, will be evaluated against preregistered equivalence bounds rather than inferred from nonsignificant difference tests alone, and content recognition will be required to reach adequate levels in both conditions rather than merely equal means. Materials are developed in English; the administration version will follow a committee translation procedure—independent translation by two bilingual translators, team reconciliation, independent back-translation, conceptual comparison for key terms, and cognitive interviews in the target language—before both versions are locked. The survey follows a fixed preregistered sequence: randomized ordering of the two Stage 1 scale blocks, the filler task, all manipulation and diagnostic checks after role threat appraisal, a funnel suspicion probe preceding demographics, and full debriefing.

## 4.4 Measures

All focal constructs use 7-point scales (1 = strongly disagree, 7 = strongly agree). All three focal scales are study-specific. Perceived organizational implementation success will be assessed with four items developed from the construct definition and the assimilation literature to capture the higher-order judgment rather than restating manipulated features (e.g., “The way Atlas has been implemented appears operationally sustainable”). Perceived role replaceability will be assessed with four items derived from the construct definition (e.g., “The main outputs of the analyst role could be produced with reduced dependence on the particular person who currently holds it”), deliberately avoiding references to job loss, headcount, or organizational intent. Role threat appraisal will be assessed with four study-specific overall primary-appraisal items developed from appraisal theory and contextualized to the implementation scenario (e.g., “In this situation, I would feel that valued aspects of my role as an analyst are threatened”); these four items constitute the sole confirmatory outcome for H2–H5. Four supplementary items referencing employment security, expertise value, autonomy, and occupational identity serve exploratory description only: they do not form a validated four-facet scale, are not averaged into the confirmatory score, and are not alternative primary outcomes. For all study-specific scales, no established validity is claimed in advance; items will undergo expert review, cognitive interviewing, and quantitative pretesting, and will be frozen before the main study. Perceived commitment credibility (three items) and the integration manipulation-check items serve the two factors; diagnostic items assess output quality, message tone, content recognition, monitoring perception, and realism.

Focal experimental and outcome items will require a response before proceeding, while optional demographic items may be skipped; scale scores will be computed as the mean of their preregistered items. If forced responding is not permitted by the ethics review, the following rules apply: when at least three of four items are completed, the scale score will be the mean of the available items; otherwise, the scale will be treated as missing for analyses requiring that construct, without triggering exclusion from other analyses; item-level missingness in CFA will be handled with full-information maximum likelihood where appropriate; and missing optional demographic data will not affect analysis eligibility. Measurement quality in the main sample will be evaluated under preregistered rules: Cronbach’s alpha and McDonald’s omega for each multi-item scale (Spearman–Brown for any two-item check), and confirmatory factor analysis comparing the hypothesized three-factor model against models combining perceived role replaceability with role threat, combining perceived role replaceability with perceived implementation success, and combining all focal items into a single factor; item deletion is permitted only as sensitivity analysis, never to improve hypothesis results. As a supplementary measurement check, multigroup CFA will examine configural, metric, and scalar invariance of the Stage 1 scales across integration conditions. Demographics and individual-difference measures appear only after the suspicion probe.

## 4.5 Pretesting and scale validation

Three preliminary studies precede the main experiment. First, cognitive interviews with target-population workers will probe whether any material is read as announcing dismissals, whether the low-integration deployment is read as unapproved or deficient, whether version tracking is read as monitoring, whether the low-substantiation message is read as negative or deceptive, and whether replaceability items are semantically distinct from threat. Second, a quantitative material pretest will verify the manipulations against the preregistered equivalence bounds described above and establish minimum plausible reading times, the expected exclusion rate, and the effect-size inputs for the power simulation. Third, an independent scale-validation sample—separate from the main experiment to avoid measurement priming—will complete the perceived role replaceability items, the perceived implementation success items, a scenario-contextualized adaptation of an established job insecurity measure (Vander Elst et al., 2014), and the role threat items; confirmatory factor analyses will compare the hypothesized four-factor model against models merging replaceability with job insecurity, with role threat, and with implementation success. STARA awareness will be retained as a generalized external correlate rather than included in the primary scenario-specific discriminant-validity CFA. The main experiment will not include the job insecurity measure.

## 4.6 Analysis strategy

Analyses will be conducted in R; software versions and packages will be reported in the final manuscript. Both factors will be effect-coded (−0.5, +0.5), with the scale-order condition (O) as a preregistered nuisance covariate and cohort fixed effects (C) included in all models to reflect blocked randomization. Cluster-robust inference will be reported as a robustness analysis only if the number of cohorts is sufficient; otherwise, small-sample corrections or wild-cluster bootstrap procedures will be used. H1a and H1b will be tested as the integration effect in regressions of each Stage 1 outcome on X, O, and C in the Stage 1 analysis sample; the two hypotheses are conceptually distinct and separately evaluated, supplemented by the correlation between the two perceptions, and support for the paradox’s initial signature requires both effects. A procedural balance check will assess whether the later-assigned substantiation condition is associated with the previously measured Stage 1 outcomes; any observed imbalance will be reported as a randomization imbalance rather than interpreted as a treatment effect.

H2 through H5 will be defined within a single second-stage moderated mediation model (Hayes, 2022) in the full-model sample: M = a₀ + a₁X + a₂O + γC + e_M, and Y = b₀ + b₁M + b₂W + b₃(M × W) + b₄X + b₅O + δC + e_Y, with M mean-centered before constructing the interaction term. With effect coding, b₁ estimates the average replaceability–threat slope (H2: b₁ > 0), b₃ the slope difference (H4: b₃ < 0), a₁b₁ the average indirect effect (H3), and a₁b₃ the index of moderated mediation (H5), each evaluated with percentile bootstrap confidence intervals (10,000 resamples). Bootstrap resampling will preserve the blocked design and any cohort dependence; unrestricted individual-level resampling will not be used when cohort-level dependence is present. The direct effect of X, the average indirect effect, the conditional indirect effects at each substantiation level, and the index of moderated mediation will be reported; a supplementary simple-mediation model will report the conventional pooled total and indirect effects. Because perceived role replaceability is measured rather than experimentally manipulated, the indirect-effect estimates will be interpreted as model-based evidence consistent with the proposed mediation mechanism, conditional on the assumption of no unmeasured mediator–outcome confounding. No full mediation is assumed. The X × O interaction is a preregistered robustness check with no data-dependent inclusion decisions.

Manipulation-check analyses will report the integration effect on the integration checks and the substantiation effect on credibility, with between-condition diagnostics for output quality, tone, content recognition, monitoring perception, and realism evaluated against the preregistered equivalence bounds; manipulation checks are not exclusion criteria, and post-manipulation diagnostics are never covariates. Suspicion-probe responses will be coded by two condition-blind coders (0 = no recognition; 1 = manipulations recognized without directional hypotheses; 2 = core hypotheses recognized), with intercoder reliability reported; main analyses retain all valid participants, and a sensitivity analysis excludes category 2. Individual-difference variables serve descriptive, balance, and robustness purposes only.

## 4.7 Ethical considerations

Approval or an exemption determination from the relevant ethics review body will be obtained before data collection, and the final manuscript will report the review category and reference number. Participation is voluntary with informed consent. Responses will initially be confidential and coded: the linkage information required for withdrawal will be stored separately from response data and destroyed after the withdrawal deadline, after which the analytic dataset will be fully de-identified; consent and debriefing materials use the same description. Because recruitment occurs in a continuing-education setting, participation will have no bearing on grades or program standing, instructors will not know who participates, recruitment materials will be distributed where feasible by personnel without evaluative authority, and compensation will be modest enough to avoid undue inducement. The debriefing discloses both manipulations, explains the initially neutral framing, and states that the company, the AI system, and all events described are entirely fictional and do not reflect participants’ own jobs, employers, or actual job security.

## A.1 Study introduction (neutral framing; identical across conditions)

This study examines how employees evaluate workplace technology arrangements. You will read a scenario about a fictional company and answer questions about your impressions. The company and the AI system described are fictional; any resemblance to real organizations or products is coincidental.

## A.2 Common introduction (identical across conditions)

Please read the following scenario carefully and imagine yourself in the situation it describes.

You work as a project analyst at Meridian Partners, a mid-sized professional services firm with about 500 employees. Your role involves receiving client information, analyzing data, preparing draft reports and recommendations, and coordinating follow-up with client teams. You have been in this role for three years, and your recent performance reviews have indicated that you are meeting expectations.

Six months ago, Meridian introduced Atlas, a company-approved internal generative AI system, for its analyst teams. Atlas can analyze data, draft summaries and reports, and answer questions about documents. In internal evaluations, its outputs have generally been rated as accurate and professional when used appropriately. Your direct supervisor supports the introduction and has encouraged the team to make good use of it. The company’s stated purpose is to improve the efficiency and quality of analytical work.

## A.3 Stage 1 — Integration manipulation

Low integration. At Meridian, Atlas is available as a stand-alone assistant that analysts can open in a separate window. Whether and when to use it is left to each analyst’s own judgment. Analysts typically turn to it for occasional, discrete tasks—summarizing a lengthy document, refining a section of text, or checking a piece of analysis. Atlas is not connected to Meridian’s client databases or reporting platforms: material an analyst wants Atlas to work on is entered manually, and results are carried back into the analyst’s own documents by hand. There are no company templates or standard procedures for using it; each analyst develops a personal way of working with the tool, and these individual practices are not documented anywhere. Atlas activity is not automatically linked to the firm’s workflow records or reporting systems. The rest of the workflow—receiving client information, structuring the analysis, assembling the report, and quality review—continues to be organized the way it was before.

High integration. At Meridian, Atlas has been built into the analyst workflow itself. Its use is a formal step at several connected stages: client information arrives through an intake system that routes it to Atlas for initial structuring; analysts then work with Atlas to develop the analysis and generate the draft report; the draft moves automatically into a quality-check stage; and the final version is archived in the company’s knowledge base. Inputs and outputs follow standardized specifications, and analysts work from a shared library of company-maintained prompt templates that define how each stage is performed. Atlas is connected to Meridian’s client databases and reporting platforms, so information flows between systems without manual transfer. Every document produced in the workflow carries version tracking, so that revisions can be traced for quality purposes. This integrated procedure is documented in the company’s official workflow manual and is now the standard way in which analyst work at Meridian is organized.

Stage 1 measures: perceived organizational implementation success and perceived role replaceability (block order randomized and recorded), followed later by Stage 1 manipulation checks per the fixed sequence.

## A.5 Stage 2 — Commitment substantiation manipulation

Common lead-in. One week later, Meridian’s management communicates with all analyst teams about how analyst roles will develop alongside Atlas.

Low substantiation. In a message to all analysts, management states the following: “Atlas is here to support our analysts, not the other way around. The company will provide retraining for analysts. Analysts will retain final approval responsibility for client recommendations. The company will introduce a senior analyst career path focused on complex judgment and client advisory work. We believe our analysts are central to Meridian’s success, and we are confident that these developments will benefit everyone. Management looks forward to taking these steps together with our analyst teams, and we appreciate the professionalism our teams bring to this change.” No policy document, implementation timetable, or assigned accountability accompanied the message.

High substantiation. In a formal announcement accompanied by a published policy document, management states the following: “Atlas is here to support our analysts, not the other way around. The company will provide retraining for analysts: the training program has an approved and allocated budget, begins next quarter, and every analyst will complete it within the year. Analysts will retain final approval responsibility for client recommendations: revised job descriptions recording this responsibility take effect in two months. The company will introduce a senior analyst career path focused on complex judgment and client advisory work: the track opens this year. The Director of Human Resources, together with each unit head, is accountable for carrying out these steps, and quarterly progress reports will be available to all employees.” The policy document recording these commitments is available on the company intranet.

## A.7 Manipulation checks and diagnostic items (7-point scales)

Commitment credibility (after role threat): CMC1 The company’s commitments about analyst roles are backed by concrete organizational actions. CMC2 The announcement includes specific, verifiable details such as budgets, timelines, and responsible managers. CMC3 Employees could check whether the company is following through on these commitments.

Integration checks (with recall prompt “Thinking back to the way Atlas was described in the first part of the scenario…”): IMC1 Use of the AI system is embedded as a formal step across multiple stages of the workflow. IMC2 Analysts’ work with the AI system follows standardized, company-maintained specifications and templates. IMC3 The AI system is connected to the company’s other internal systems. IMC4 Each analyst decides individually whether and when to use the AI system. (reverse)

Diagnostics (all after the primary outcome): CC1 The AI system described in the scenario produces high-quality output. CC3 The tone of the company’s message toward analysts is positive. CC4 In its message, the company mentions retraining, decision responsibilities, and career paths. MP1 The tracking described in the scenario would make analysts feel monitored. MP2 The workflow would make analysts’ individual activity more visible to management. RC1 The situation described in this study could happen in a real company.

Funnel suspicion probe (open-ended, wide to narrow): (1) What do you think this study was about? (2) What do you think the researchers were trying to examine? (3) Did you notice anything about how Atlas was used at the company? Please explain. (4) Did you notice anything about management’s message to analysts? Please explain. (5) Did you form any expectations about the study’s hypotheses? If so, what were they?

Debriefing (after the probe) discloses both manipulations, explains the initially neutral framing, states that the company, the AI system, and all events described were entirely fictional and do not reflect participants’ own jobs, employers, or actual job security, and describes the confidential, coded, and subsequently de-identified handling of responses with the withdrawal procedure and deadline.

## A.8 Preregistered exclusion rules (operationalized)

Attention: failure to select “Not stated” on the instructed-response item. Comprehension: fewer than two of four filler items correct, judged on the four-item total, never on a single error. Extreme speed: scenario-page reading time below the pretest-derived minimum (fifth percentile of check-passing pretest readers) combined with total completion time below one-third of the sample median; each criterion also applied singly in sensitivity analyses; thresholds fixed before inspecting main-study data. Duplicate/invalid: repeated panel or participant IDs, identical open-ended responses, platform-flagged invalid records, with device identification used only where platform and ethics rules permit. Exclusion reasons may be multiply coded; unique excluded participants are counted once. Manipulation-check and diagnostic-check scores are never exclusion criteria.
